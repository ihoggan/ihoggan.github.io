The lean toolkit

For years I humped a heavy box of tools from job to job, and still reached into it for the wrong thing. The box wasn't the problem. I'd built it around tools, not around the jobs that actually happen in a house. This is how I'm rebuilding it: look first, make it safe, log the fault, and keep three small kits instead of one big one.

Part 1

The best tool is a look

Most bad repairs start with grabbing a tool. A dripping tap gets a spanner before anyone has worked out whether it's the washer, the valve or the joint below it. So my first rule costs nothing: stop and look.

  1. What exactly is wrong? Say it in one sentence. "Water drips from the spout when the tap is off" is a fault. "The tap's broken" isn't.
  2. Is anything unsafe right now? Water near electrics, a smell of gas, a hot or burning smell from a socket. If so, Part 2 comes before anything else.
  3. Is it mine to fix? Some jobs are DIY, some are for a qualified person by law or by common sense. Knowing the difference is a skill too.
Lesson

Five minutes of looking saves an hour of undoing. The diagnosis is the job; the tool is just the last step.

Part 2

Know your three off-switches

Before you need them, find these, and show everyone in the house:

WaterThe stopcock, usually under the kitchen sink or where the pipe comes in. Turn it clockwise to close. Try it once now: old ones seize.
ElectricityThe consumer unit (fuse box). The main switch turns everything off; each trip switch covers one circuit. Label them if they aren't.
GasThe lever by the gas meter: turn it so it's across the pipe to shut off. If you smell gas, don't touch switches, open windows, get out, and call the free gas emergency line, 0800 111 999.
Where DIY stops

Resetting a trip switch or changing a plug fuse is fine. New circuits, new sockets in kitchens and bathrooms, and work in the consumer unit are for a registered electrician. Anything on a gas appliance or pipe is only for a Gas Safe registered engineer. No exceptions, no "just this once".

Part 3

Log the fault before you fix it

I borrowed this from work on computers: write the fault down as open before you start, and mark it closed when it's fixed. It sounds fussy. It isn't.

date        what                              status   notes
2026-11-02  kitchen tap drips when off        OPEN     washer? check model first
2026-11-03  kitchen tap drips when off        CLOSED   new washer, 1/2" — spare in pouch
  • You stop half-fixing things and forgetting.
  • The notes tell you what worked, and what part it took, next time.
  • Patterns show up: the same circuit tripping every week isn't bad luck. It's a fault to report.

A notebook in the kit is enough. Mine will end up in a small database at home, but paper never runs out of battery.

Part 4

Three kits, not one heavy box

1. Grab pouchSmall enough to carry to any room in one hand. The first response to nearly every household fault: torch, a screwdriver with interchangeable bits, small adjustable spanner, pliers, utility knife, tape (electrical and duct), gloves, a pencil, the fault log.
2. Job kitFetched once you know what the job is. A small hammer, drill and bits, a spirit level, tape measure, sealant, a few fixings, a bucket and old towels.
3. The storeEverything else, in the garage or a cupboard: the big tools, spares, and the things you use twice a year. Sorted so you can find them, not carried around.

The test of the grab pouch: can you deal with the first five minutes of any common fault without going back for something? If you keep fetching the same thing, it moves into the pouch. If something in the pouch hasn't been used in a year, it moves out.

Weight matters indoors too. My old claw hammer has lasted 35 years and still works perfectly, but it's far too heavy for hanging a picture in a plastered wall. A small ball-pein hammer does every indoor job I have, and the big one stays in the store for the jobs that need it.

Part 6

An afternoon to rebuild yours

  1. Empty your toolbox onto a sheet. All of it.
  2. Write down the last ten jobs you actually did around the house.
  3. For each job, pick out the tools it really took. That pile is your grab pouch and job kit.
  4. Everything else goes to the store, sorted. Duplicates and broken tools go to a charity shop or the tip.
  5. Find your three off-switches, try the stopcock, and label the consumer unit.
  6. Start a fault log, and write the first entry today.
Check

You can carry your grab pouch in one hand, and you know where all three off-switches are.

What I'd want you to take away

Lean is a habit, not a shopping list

Look first

Say the fault in one sentence before you pick up a tool.

Make it safe

Know your stopcock, your consumer unit and your gas lever, and where DIY stops.

Log it

Open when you start, closed when it's done, and a note for next time.
