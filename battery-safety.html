Living safely with lithium batteries

A power-cut plan leans on lithium batteries: power banks, rechargeable torches, the phone in your pocket. They're brilliant, and they're safe when they're treated right. UK fire services keep seeing the same few causes of battery fires, which means a handful of habits cover nearly all the risk. Here they are, plus the maths of how many phone charges a power bank really holds.

Part 1

Six habits

These come straight from UK fire and rescue service advice. Each one closes off a common way battery fires start.

1. The right chargerUse the charger that came with it, or a genuine replacement from a trusted seller. Cheap counterfeit chargers and batteries may not meet UK safety standards.
2. Charge while awakeCharge when you're at home, awake and nearby. Unplug when it's full. Not overnight, and not while you're out.
3. A hard surfaceOn a hard, flat surface that won't burn: not a bed, sofa or carpet, and never covered by anything.
4. Not on the way outNever charge in a hallway or by the door you'd use to escape. If it did catch fire, it would block the way out.
5. Not too hot, not too coldAway from radiators, direct sunlight and freezing sheds or cars, for charging and for storing.
6. A smoke alarm nearbyEspecially where bigger batteries charge, such as e-bikes, e-scooters and large power stations.
Look around now

Where does each battery in your home charge? Bedside table, sofa arm, the hall by the front door? Which of the six habits does each one break?

Part 2

Three warning signs

Stop using a battery, and the device it's in, if it is:

  • Swollen. A phone screen lifting, a power bank that won't sit flat, a case that's bulging. That's gas building up inside.
  • Damaged. Dropped hard, dented, punctured or crushed, even if it still seems to work.
  • Overheating. Hot to hold while charging or in use, or smelling of burning or chemicals.

Don't charge it again, and don't throw it in a bin. Put it somewhere it can't burn anything, away from doors and away from anything flammable, and take it to a recycling point (Part 4) as soon as you can.

If one catches fire

Battery fires are fierce and can reignite. Don't try to fight it. Get everyone out, close the door behind you, and call 999.

Part 3

Loose cells need extra care

Torches and many gadgets use loose rechargeable cells, often the 18650 size, about the size of a fat AA. A loose cell has bare metal at both ends. If it touches keys or coins in a pocket or bag, it can short-circuit and get very hot, very quickly.

  • Carry and store loose cells in a plastic cell case, never loose in a pocket or a drawer of odds and ends.
  • Buy from reputable sellers. Unusually cheap cells with unusually big capacity numbers are a warning, not a bargain.
  • Charge them in a proper charger made for that cell type.
  • If the plastic wrap on a cell is torn or split, stop using it. That wrap is part of its insulation.

Part 4

Getting rid of them

Never put a lithium battery in the household rubbish or recycling bin. Bin lorries and recycling plants catch fire from them every year.

  • Look for the crossed-out wheelie bin symbol: it means "not in the bin".
  • Put tape over the ends (or the terminals), so they can't short.
  • Take them to a battery recycling bin at a supermarket or shop, or to your council's household waste recycling centre.
  • If the battery can't come out of the device, recycle the whole device as electrical waste.
  • Keep old batteries somewhere cool and dry until you go, and don't mix them with other battery types.

Part 5

Power bank maths

A "10,000 mAh" power bank does not charge a 5,000 mAh phone twice. Two things eat into it. The number on the bank is measured at the cell's own voltage, but it delivers power at a different voltage. And some energy is lost as heat in the conversion and the charging. In practice, roughly 60 to 70% of the rated energy ends up in your phone.

Assumes 3.7 V cells in the bank, a 3.85 V phone battery, about 65% getting through to a phone, and about 85% to a USB torch. Look for watt-hours (Wh) on the label: it's the honest number.
The sums

Energy in watt-hours = mAh × volts ÷ 1000. So 10,000 mAh × 3.7 V = 37 Wh in the bank. A 4,500 mAh phone at 3.85 V holds about 17 Wh. With about 65% getting through: 37 × 0.65 ÷ 17 ≈ 1.4 charges, not 2.2. Plan on that, and you won't be caught short.

For a long power cut: a phone on power saving, with the screen dimmed and used for calls and messages, can last days on one charge plus a power bank. Keep the bank topped up, between about half and full, rather than empty in a drawer.

Part 6

A quick audit

Check

Nothing charges on a bed, a sofa, or in the hallway.

Check

Every charger is the original or a genuine replacement.

Check

Loose cells live in cases, and anything swollen or damaged is out of use and waiting for recycling.

Check

You know where your nearest battery recycling point is.

What I'd want you to take away

Respect, not fear

Charge where you can see it

Awake, nearby, on a hard surface, and never by the way out.

Swollen means stop

Damaged, swollen or hot: stop using it, and recycle it properly.

Read the watt-hours

About two-thirds of a power bank's energy reaches your phone. Plan on that.
