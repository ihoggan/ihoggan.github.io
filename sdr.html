See what's in the air

Right now, hundreds of radio signals are passing through the room you're in. A USB dongle costing about the same as a takeaway, plus free software on Ubuntu, lets you see them: a live picture of the radio spectrum, from FM stations to amateur radio. This guide sets it up, and tells you honestly what UK law lets you listen to.

Before you start

What you need, and the law

  • An RTL-SDR dongle: a USB stick with a small aerial, sold for roughly £20 to £35. Look for "RTL-SDR" and the chip name RTL2832U.
  • A computer running Ubuntu, and a terminal. Meet the terminal covers everything you'll need. I run mine on nix5, my Ubuntu laptop.
  • A window. Radio signals come through walls, but an aerial near a window hears far more.
What you may listen to in the UK

You don't need a licence to own or use a receiver. But Ofcom's guidance is that receivers are for "general reception", which means licensed broadcast stations and hobby radio such as amateur radio. Deliberately listening to messages that weren't meant for you is an offence under section 48 of the Wireless Telegraphy Act 2006, and so is passing on anything you overhear.

So this guide sticks to FM broadcast, amateur radio, and looking at the spectrum. The band explorer in step 5 marks what's yours to listen to. When in doubt, Ofcom's own advice is to get legal advice, and mine is simply to leave it alone. See Ofcom: rules on using radio equipment.

Step 1

What a software-defined radio is

An ordinary radio is built for one job. The circuits that tune to a station and turn the signal into sound are fixed in hardware. In a software-defined radio, or SDR, the hardware does as little as possible: it grabs a slice of the spectrum and hands it to your computer as numbers. Everything else, the tuning, the filtering, the turning into sound, is done by a program.

The dongle's story is a happy accident. The RTL2832U chip was made for cheap USB digital TV sticks. Around 2012, people discovered it could be put into a mode that sends the raw radio data to the computer, and a very cheap radio scanner was born.

Think about it

If software does the decoding, what would it take to receive a completely different kind of signal with the same dongle?

The answer

Only different software. The same stick can show FM radio in one program and a map of the spectrum in another. That's the whole point, and why a £25 dongle has kept hobbyists busy for over a decade.

Check

You can explain in a sentence what the dongle does and what the software does.

Step 2

Plug it in, and ask the computer what it sees

Screw the aerial on, plug the dongle into a USB port, then list everything on the USB bus:

$ lsusb
Predict first

The dongle was made for TV. How do you think it will describe itself?

What you should see

A line like Realtek Semiconductor Corp. RTL2838 DVB-T. DVB-T is digital TV. It still thinks it's a TV stick, and that matters in the next step.

Check

lsusb lists a Realtek RTL2838 device. If nothing new appears, try another USB port and run it again.

Step 3

Install the tools, and fix the one thing that goes wrong

Two packages: rtl-sdr gives you command-line tools for the dongle, and gqrx-sdr is a radio program with a picture of the spectrum.

$ sudo apt install rtl-sdr gqrx-sdr
$ rtl_test

rtl_test checks the dongle and keeps running until you stop it with Ctrl + C. Read the first few lines before stopping it. There are two outcomes.

It works

You'll see Found 1 device(s), then a line naming the tuner, such as Found Rafael Micro R820T tuner or Found Elonics E4000 tuner. The tuner decides which frequencies you can reach: the common R820T covers about 24 MHz to 1.7 GHz. Mine has the older E4000, which covers roughly 52 MHz to 2.2 GHz, with a gap in the middle.

It complains

Kernel driver is active, or device is claimed by second instance of librtlsdr.
In the first case, please either detach or blacklist the kernel module
(dvb_usb_rtl28xxu), or enable automatic detaching at compile time.
usb_claim_interface error -6
Read the error

Don't search for a fix yet. Read the message. Remember what lsusb called the dongle. What do you think has happened?

What happened, and the fix

Ubuntu saw a TV stick and loaded its TV driver, dvb_usb_rtl28xxu. That driver grabbed the dongle first, so the radio tools can't have it. The message even names the driver. The fix is to tell Ubuntu never to load it (a "blacklist"), unload it now, then unplug and replug the dongle:

$ echo 'blacklist dvb_usb_rtl28xxu' | sudo tee /etc/modprobe.d/blacklist-rtl.conf
$ sudo modprobe -r dvb_usb_rtl28xxu
$ rtl_test

Error messages are usually trying to help. This one tells you the cause and the name of the thing to fix. The habit of reading them first will save you hours on every project.

Check

rtl_test finds your device and names its tuner, and Ctrl + C stops it.

Step 4

Tune in an FM station

Start Gqrx from Show Apps, or type gqrx in the terminal. The first time, it asks which device to use: choose the one mentioning Realtek or RTL2838, and click OK.

  1. Click the power button at the top left to start receiving.
  2. In the Receiver Options panel, set Mode to WFM (stereo). That's "wide FM", which is what broadcast stations use.
  3. Set the frequency to somewhere between 88 and 108 MHz. BBC Radio 2 is between 88 and 91 MHz across most of the UK.
  4. In Input Controls, raise the gain until stations stand out clearly from the background.

The top half of the window is the spectrum: signal strength across a range of frequencies. The scrolling picture beneath it is the waterfall, the same thing over time. Each FM station is a bright vertical stripe about 200 kHz wide.

Experiment

Click on a bright stripe to tune to it. Now try: turn the gain right up, and then right down. At which setting do stations sound best, and why isn't it the maximum?

What was going on

Too little gain and weak stations vanish into the background hiss. Too much and strong stations overload the dongle: ghost copies of stations appear where there are none, and everything gets noisier. The best setting is the lowest gain that makes the stations you want stand clear. It's rarely the top of the scale.

Check

You're listening to an FM station, and you've found a gain setting where stations are clear without ghosts.

Step 5

What else is in the air

FM radio is a tiny slice of what's out there. Slide through the spectrum below to see what lives where in the UK, and whether it's yours to listen to.

Amateur radio is the best next stop. Licensed amateurs chat to each other on purpose, in public, and it's hobby radio: exactly what receivers are for. Try the 2-metre band around 145 MHz, set the mode to Narrow FM, and wait. Many areas have a repeater, a relay station on a hill, where local amateurs talk in the evenings.

Check

You've found at least one signal outside the FM band, and you can say which bands are yours to listen to and which aren't.

Step 6

Look without listening: map the spectrum

You don't have to listen to anything to learn a lot. The dongle's own tools can sweep a range of frequencies and record how busy each one is, over hours. This scans the FM band every 10 seconds for an hour and saves the results:

$ rtl_power -f 88M:108M:125k -i 10 -e 1h fm-scan.csv

The numbers say: from 88 to 108 MHz, in 125 kHz slices, averaging over 10 seconds (-i), for one hour (-e). The result is a spreadsheet file: one row per sweep, one column per slice. Open it in LibreOffice Calc, or plot it with Python.

Predict first

If you ran a scan over the 2-metre amateur band from teatime to midnight, when would you expect it to be busiest? Run it and find out:

$ rtl_power -f 144M:146M:12.5k -i 60 -e 6h two-metres.csv

This is the kind of question I like: a guess, a measurement, and usually a surprise. It's also how I'm planning to watch for thunderstorms, since lightning makes a burst of radio noise that shows up as a flash across the whole waterfall.

Check

You've recorded a scan with rtl_power and opened the file to find when a band was busiest.

That's it

What I'd want you to take away

The software is the radio

The dongle only listens. Everything else is programs, so a new use is usually just new software.

Read the error first

The driver clash told you its own cause and fix. Most errors will, if you read them before searching.

Know what's yours

Broadcast and hobby radio are for everyone. Other people's messages aren't. Looking at the spectrum is where the learning is anyway.
