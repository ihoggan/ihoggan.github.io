Part 2

How heavy are penguins? A histogram

A histogram sorts values into bins and draws a bar for how many land in each. It shows the shape of a column, which an average never can.

Predict first

The average weight is about 4.2 kg. Sketch on paper what you think the histogram will look like. One hump in the middle? Something else?

What the shape is telling you

It isn't one hump. There's a big crowd around 3.5 to 4 kg and a second, smaller hump around 5 to 5.5 kg. When a histogram has two humps, it's often two different groups mixed together. From step 2 you can guess which: the heavy Gentoos, and everyone else. The average, 4.2 kg, falls between the two humps: a weight that describes neither group very well.

Experiment

Change bins=25 to bins=5, then to bins=80. Do the two humps survive? Too few bins hide the shape; too many turn it into noise.

A cloud that slopes up from bottom left to top right: that's what a strong positive correlation looks like. Now give each species its colour:

Look closely

Part 4

The pattern that turns upside down

Now the bills. Each penguin's bill has a length (how far it sticks out) and a depth (how tall it is, top to bottom). First, all the penguins together, in grey:

A slight downward drift, and a correlation of about −0.23: across all the penguins, longer bills tend to be shallower.

Predict first

Now colour it by species. Within one species, do you think longer bills will still be shallower?

What just happened

Inside every species, the slope goes up: longer bills are deeper, with correlations of +0.39, +0.65 and +0.65. The overall downward drift only appeared because the three groups sit in different places: Gentoos have long but shallow bills, and Adelies short but deep ones. Mix them together and the groups' positions swamp the pattern inside each one.

This has a name: Simpson's paradox. It's the same family of trap as step 2's heavy island, and it's the strongest argument for drawing your data before you trust a single number about it.

Lesson

A correlation for everyone can point the opposite way to the correlation for every group inside it. Always ask: what happens if I colour it in?

Look at the coloured chart again, though. Something else is true here: on these two measurements, all three species sit in their own patches, Adelie and Chinstrap included. That's exactly what step 4 needs.

Part 5

Your turn: find the best pair

Challenge

You have four measurements: bill_length_mm, bill_depth_mm, flipper_length_mm and body_mass_g. Which pair separates all three species most cleanly? Copy the coloured scatter box into the empty one below and swap the two column names. Try at least three pairs.

What most people find

Bill length is the star. Paired with bill depth, or with flipper length, it puts each species in its own corner, because it's the one measurement where Chinstraps clearly differ from Adelies. Flipper length against body mass, the pair from Part 3, keeps Adelies and Chinstraps mixed up. In step 4, a model will be given all four measurements and will have to discover this for itself.

Part 6

Making charts that don't lie

Every chart on this page has labels with units, a title, and a key when colours mean something. That's not decoration. A chart without them makes the reader guess, and guesses go wrong. Three habits worth keeping:

  • Label the axes, with units. "Mass" means nothing; "Body mass (kg)" can't be misread.
  • Watch where the axis starts. A bar chart whose axis starts at 3.5 kg instead of zero can make a small difference look enormous. Try it: plt.bar(["Adelie", "Gentoo"], [3.7, 5.1]); plt.ylim(3.5, 5.2).
  • Use colour for meaning, and keep it consistent. Here, each species keeps the same colour on every chart.
Check

You can draw a histogram and a scatter plot, colour points by group with a legend, and explain why the bill pattern reversed.

On your own machine

Run it without the browser

If you set up the folder in step 1, matplotlib is already installed. In a plain terminal, add plt.show() at the end of each chart to open it in a window, or save it as a picture instead:

cd ~/code/data-path && source venv/bin/activate
python3
# ... a chart's lines, then:
plt.savefig("bills.png", dpi=150)    # writes bills.png in the folder
plt.close()

What I'd want you to take away

Shape beats average

The histogram's two humps showed what 4.2 kg hid: two groups, and almost nobody in the middle.

Colour it in

The bill pattern reversed inside every species. Draw the groups before you trust a number about everyone.

Pictures pick features

The right pair of measurements separated all three species. Step 4 hands that job to a model.
