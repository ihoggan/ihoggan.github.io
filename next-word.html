Guess the next word

Every AI chatbot does one thing, over and over: it guesses the next word. On this page you'll build the simplest machine that can do that, out of nothing but counting. You'll watch it write, and then you'll find the exact place where it breaks. Fixing that break is what the real thing is for.

Before you start

What you need

A web browser. That's all. Everything on this page runs on your own device: the panels are real working machines, not animations, and nothing you type is sent anywhere.

I wrote this after building a language model from nothing in Python, one small piece at a time, on a 2015 desktop PC. This page is the first thing I wish someone had shown me before I started.

Step 1

The whole job is one guess

Finish this sentence: the cat sat on the …

You had an answer before you reached the end. You didn't look anything up. You have read enough English to know what tends to come next. A language model does exactly that, and only that: given some words, it guesses the next one. To write a paragraph it guesses a word, adds it on, and guesses again.

So what does a machine need in order to guess? Something to have read. Here is a very small world for ours: thirty sentences about a cat, a dog, a mouse and a bird. This is everything our machine will ever know.

You can change that text whenever you like, and every panel on this page will follow. Leave it alone for now.

Predict first

Read down the sentences. Without counting: which word comes after the most often? And what comes after sat?

Hold on to your two answers. The next step checks them.

Check

You've read the tiny world, and written down (or at least decided on) your two guesses.

Step 2

Count what follows

Here is the simplest way a machine could guess. Go through the text once. Every time you meet a word, make a tally mark for the word that came straight after it. When you've finished you have a table: for each word, what followed it and how often.

The machine below has done that. Tap a word to see its row of the table.

The counting table

Were your guesses right? Notice that the table doesn't give one answer. It gives a list of answers, each with a share. Those shares are the machine's probabilities: how likely it thinks each next word is.

One piece of jargon, because you'll meet it everywhere. The machine doesn't deal in words exactly. It deals in tokens: the pieces the text is cut into. Here a token is a word or a full stop. Tap the full stop in the panel and you'll see it has a row of its own.

Predict first

Which word is the machine most sure about what follows, and which is it least sure about? Decide, then tap through the words and look at how the bars are spread.

What I see

With my text, plenty of words leave no doubt at all. on, under, chased and ate are followed by the every single time. So is the full stop, because every sentence here starts with the.

The word it's least sure about is the itself. Thirteen different things can follow it, and even the favourite has less than a quarter of the tally. That's worth remembering. A language model is never choosing "the right word". It is always weighing a list.

Check

You can say what most often follows the, and you've found a word whose next word is never in doubt.

Step 3

Let it write

A table of guesses is already enough to write with. Start with a word. Look up its row. Pick a next word. Now look up that word's row, and go round again.

There's one decision to make each time: which word to pick from the row. The obvious choice is the favourite, the one with the longest bar.

Predict first

The panel below is set to Always the favourite. Before you press anything: what do you think it will write if you keep pressing Next word twenty times?

The writer · looks back one word

What happens, and why

It goes round in a circle: the cat sat on the cat sat on the cat sat on… for ever. After the the favourite is cat, after cat it's sat, after sat it's on, after on it's the, and now we're back where we began. Always taking the favourite means always taking the same road.

Now switch to Roll the dice and press Start again, then Write 15 more a few times. The dice is loaded: a word with a bar twice as long comes up twice as often. But the short bars get their turn.

Read what the dice writes. Hardly any of it is in my text word for word: I had it write three thousand sentences, and only about one in twenty-five was a copy. The rest are sentences that nobody wrote: the bird sat on the cheese, the dog ate the wall, or just the mat. The machine has made new sentences out of old tallies. Some are sensible and some are nonsense, and the machine has no idea which is which.

That is how every chatbot writes: one loaded roll of the dice per token. The setting usually called temperature is how heavily the dice is loaded towards the favourite. It's also why the same question can get you two different answers.

Check

You've seen the favourite-only writer get stuck in a loop, and the dice write at least one sentence that isn't in the text.

Step 4

Give it more to go on

The writer in step 3 only ever looked at one word: the last one. That's why it could write the dog ate the wall. By the time it reached the second "the", it had forgotten there was a dog, and that anything was being eaten.

The obvious fix is to let it look back further. Count what follows each pair of words, or each run of three. The panel below lets you choose. It can also read a bigger text: the opening of Shakespeare's Coriolanus, about 5,400 words. That's the start of the same file I trained my own model on.

Predict first

Stay on the tiny world. You'll set it to look back 3 words and write a few sentences. Will its writing get better, or worse? And in what way?

What I see

It gets more sensible, and far less its own. I had it write three thousand sentences at each setting and checked them against my thirty. Looking back one word, 4% were straight copies of a sentence of mine. Two words, 46%. Three words, 81%, and the few new ones were small swaps like the bird sat on the rug. No more dogs eating walls. But almost nothing that wasn't already there.

Try the same on Shakespeare. Looking back one word you get a heap of real words in no order, and about one word in six is grey. Two words gives phrases that almost sound like something. Three words, and about nine words in ten are grey: it is quoting the play back at you.

Now break it

Go back to the tiny world and look back 2 words. In the Start with box type the dog climbed and press Next word.

Predict first

The text never says a dog climbed anything. But it does say the cat climbed the tree and the cat climbed the wall. What do you think the machine will do?

What it does

It stops dead. It has never met "dog climbed", so that row of its table is empty, and an empty row is no help at all. Switch it to look back 1 word and it carries on happily, because it has met "climbed".

You or I would have said the tree without thinking. We know a dog is the same sort of thing as a cat, so what cats climb is a fair guess for a dog. The counting machine can't make that step. To it, "cat" and "dog" are two unrelated marks on a page. Everything it knows about one is useless for the other.

Check

You've made it recite by looking back 3 words, and you've stopped it dead with a start it had never met.

Step 5

The wall, measured

So there's a trade. Look back a little and the machine forgets what it was talking about. Look back a lot and it can only repeat, and it's helpless the moment anything is new. Is that a quirk of thirty sentences about a cat? It's easy to measure on the real text.

The test is the one I used on every model I built. Hide the last tenth of the text. Build the table from the rest. Then walk through the hidden part and ask, at every word: has the machine ever met this situation before?

Situations in the hidden tenth that it had met before

Predict first

Look at how fast the bars fall. What would fix it? One answer is "read more text". How much more do you think it would take?

My answer

More than exists. Every extra word of looking back multiplies the number of possible situations by the size of the vocabulary. English in everyday use has tens of thousands of words. Take a modest 10,000:

Look back 110,000 situations to have met
Look back 2100 million
Look back 3a million million
Look back 10a 1 followed by 40 noughts

Most of those would never turn up, but the ones that do run far beyond anything that could be collected. And a chatbot has to keep hold of what you said hundreds or thousands of words ago, not ten. Counting can't get there. No amount of text makes a table that big fill up.

So the machine we actually need is one that can make a sensible guess in a situation it has never met. It has to notice that "dog" behaves like "cat", so that what it learned about one carries over to the other. And it has to look a long way back without needing a table row for every possible history.

That machine is called a transformer, and it's what sits inside the AI chatbots you've heard of. The first thing it does differently is the answer to the cat-and-dog problem: it stops treating words as marks, and turns each one into a short list of numbers that it learns, so that similar words end up with similar numbers. That's the next step on this path, and I'm writing it now.

The counting table doesn't go away, though. It's the best yardstick there is. When I trained my first model on Shakespeare, I set a counting table beside it, one that looks back three letters. My model lost. It took a better way of cutting up the text, and then a bigger model, before the transformer pulled clear. If a clever machine can't beat counting, it hasn't learned much, and it's worth knowing that before anyone tells you how clever it is.

Check

You can explain why looking back further doesn't rescue the counting machine, and what a better machine would have to be able to do.

That's it

What I'd want you to take away

It only ever guesses the next token

An essay, an answer, a poem: each is one guess, added on, then another. There is no plan being followed underneath.

It rolls a loaded dice

The model gives every possible next token a share, and one is drawn. That's why answers vary, and why "always the favourite" writes in circles.

Counting can't cope with new

A table knows only what it has met. The whole point of a real language model is to guess well where it has met nothing.
