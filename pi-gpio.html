Hand the switch to a computer

In the electronics guide your finger flicked the switch. Here, a Raspberry Pi does it, with five lines of Python. Then you'll add a button, find a bug you can actually see, and learn the one rule that stops you killing your Pi.

Before you start

What you need

  • Any Raspberry Pi with the 40-pin header, running Raspberry Pi OS. I run several, all with their pins in the same place.
  • The breadboard, LED, resistors and jumper wires from Electronics part one. You'll need female-to-male jumper wires to reach from the Pi's pins to the breadboard.
  • A small push button that fits a breadboard, for step 5.
  • A terminal on the Pi, either on its own screen or over SSH. Meet the terminal covers the basics.
Two rules for the whole guide

Shut the Pi down and unplug it before changing any wiring. One stray wire touching the wrong pin while it's running can damage it. sudo shutdown now, wait for the green light to stop flickering, then pull the power.

The pins are 3.3 volt only. Step 6 explains why this matters so much. Until then: never connect anything to the pins marked 5V except where this guide says so, and it never will.

Step 1

Meet the header

The two rows of pins along the edge of the Pi are its GPIO header: general-purpose input and output. Some pins supply power, some are ground (the battery's −, in electronics-guide terms), and most can be switched on and off by your programs. Tap any pin below.

5 volt power 3.3 volt power Ground GPIO GPIO with a second job Reserved

Pins have two numbers, and mixing them up is the most common mistake. The physical number is its position, 1 to 40. The GPIO number is its name to the Pi's chip. Physical pin 11 is GPIO17. Your code uses the GPIO number.

On the Pi itself, type this. It draws the board and the header, with pin 1 marked:

$ pinout
Check

You've found physical pin 1 on your actual Pi, and you can point to pin 11 (GPIO17) and pin 9 (ground).

Step 2

Wire the LED, choosing the resistor yourself

It's the same circuit as in the electronics guide, with a Pi pin as the battery. When the program switches GPIO17 on, that pin gives out 3.3 volts.

Use Ohm's law

The pin gives 3.3 V. A red LED takes about 2 V. A Pi pin shouldn't be asked for much current: aim for about 5 mA. What resistor do you need?

Work it through

The resistor takes what the LED doesn't: 3.3 − 2 = 1.3 V. For 5 mA, which is 0.005 A: R = 1.3 ÷ 0.005 = 260 Ω. The nearest common size above that is 330 Ω, giving about 4 mA. Bigger is the safe direction: it just makes the LED a little dimmer.

With the Pi off and unplugged:

Pin 11 (GPIO17)to one end of the 330 Ω resistor
Resistor, other endto the LED's long leg
LED short legto pin 9 (ground)

Check each wire against the map above before powering up. Two wires, one resistor, one LED: it's small enough to check properly.

Check

You chose the resistor with Ohm's law, and the circuit matches the table. The Pi is back on, and the LED is off, because nothing has told the pin to switch on yet.

Step 3

Blink it from Python

The library that talks to the pins, GPIO Zero, comes installed on Raspberry Pi OS. Make a folder for this guide and a file for the program:

$ mkdir ~/gpio && cd ~/gpio
$ nano blink.py

Type this in, then save with Ctrl + O, Enter, and leave with Ctrl + X:

from gpiozero import LED
from time import sleep

led = LED(17)          # GPIO17, which is physical pin 11

while True:
    led.on()
    sleep(1)           # wait one second
    led.off()
    sleep(1)
$ python3 blink.py

The LED blinks, once a second, forever. while True means "keep doing this until stopped". To stop it, press Ctrl + C, the terminal's stop key.

It doesn't blink

Most likely, in this order: the LED is in backwards (turn it round), the wire is on the wrong pin (count again from pin 1, or check pinout), or the number in the code is the physical pin, 11, rather than the GPIO number, 17.

If you use Python virtual environments, make yours with python3 -m venv --system-site-packages venv, so it can see the GPIO Zero that came with the system.

Check

The LED blinks once a second, and Ctrl + C stops it.

Step 4

Make it yours

You now control a real object with code, and the fastest way to understand the code is to change it. Try each of these before looking at the answers:

  1. Make it blink twice as fast.
  2. Make it flash briefly, like a lighthouse: on for a fifth of a second, off for two.
  3. Make it blink exactly ten times, then stop by itself.
Answers

1. Change both sleep(1) lines to sleep(0.5).

2. sleep(0.2) after led.on(), and sleep(2) after led.off().

3. Replace while True: with for i in range(10):. A for loop runs a set number of times; while True never stops on its own.

Check

You've made at least two of the three changes work yourself.

Step 5

Add a button, and meet a bug you can see

So far the Pi has only given out signals. Now it reads one. Shut down, unplug, and add a push button:

Pin 13 (GPIO27)to one side of the button
Pin 14 (ground)to the other side

No resistor this time. GPIO Zero switches on one built into the Pi, which holds the pin "high" until the button connects it to ground. Power up, then make button.py:

from gpiozero import Button, LED
from signal import pause

button = Button(27)    # GPIO27, physical pin 13
led = LED(17)
presses = 0

def pressed():
    global presses
    presses += 1
    led.toggle()
    print("Presses so far:", presses)

button.when_pressed = pressed
pause()                # wait here for button presses, until Ctrl + C
$ python3 button.py
Experiment

Press the button twenty times, slowly, one press at a time. Does the count always go up by exactly one? Does the LED always change?

What was going on

With many buttons, some presses count twice, or three times, and the LED seems to ignore you, because it toggled an even number of times. Your code isn't wrong. The button is. Inside it, two bits of metal slam together and bounce apart and back several times in a few thousandths of a second. The Pi is fast enough to see every bounce as a separate press.

The fix is to ignore any press that comes too soon after the last one. Change the button line to:

button = Button(27, bounce_time=0.05)   # ignore bounces within 50 ms

Your button might be a good one and never bounce. Try a cheaper one, or press lightly and quickly, and you'll usually catch it.

Check

Each press toggles the LED and adds exactly one to the count, including with bounce_time added.

Step 6

The 3.3 volt rule

The Pi's chip works at 3.3 volts. Its GPIO pins can give out 3.3 V, and they can read up to 3.3 V. They are not built to take 5 volts. Put 5 V into a GPIO pin and you can damage that pin, or the whole Pi, instantly and for good.

It's easy to do by accident, because the header has two 5V pins (2 and 4) right next to the ones you use, and many cheap sensor boards are made for 5 V systems and send 5 V signals back.

Use what you know

You have a sensor that sends back a 5 V signal, and you want to read it on a GPIO pin. Using only resistors, and what you learned about how voltage is shared round a loop, how could you make it safe?

One answer

A voltage divider. Put two resistors in a row between the sensor's signal and ground, and take your GPIO wire from the point between them. The 5 V is shared between the two resistors in proportion to their sizes. With 1 kΩ on top and 2 kΩ underneath, the lower one gets two-thirds of the 5 V: about 3.3 V. Safe.

That's the same "the shares always add up to the whole" you found with the LED and resistor. It's also why electronics is worth learning before the programming: this is how you avoid killing the computer.

Check

You can say which two header pins are 5 V, and why a 5 V signal must never go straight into a GPIO pin.

That's it

What I'd want you to take away

Two numbers per pin

Physical position for your wires, GPIO number for your code. Most "it doesn't work" moments are these two mixed up.

Real things are messy

The button bounced, and your code was right all along. Hardware bugs look like software bugs until you understand the hardware.

3.3 volts, always

Check the voltage of anything before it touches a GPIO pin, and power off before rewiring.
