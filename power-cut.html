When the lights go out

Most power cuts in the UK last minutes, a few last hours, and once in a while one lasts days. None of them need to be frightening if you've thought about it once, calmly, beforehand. This is my plan: what to do in the first ten minutes, how to get through the first night, and what matters if it goes on. No fear, no gadgets you don't need, and every safety figure from official UK guidance, linked at the end.

Part 1

The first ten minutes

  1. Torch first, not candles. Keep one where you can find it in the dark, the same place every time.
  2. Is it just you? Look out of the window. Are the street lights and the neighbours' lights out too? If it's only your home, check your fuse box: a tripped switch is the usual cause.
  3. Call 105. It's free, it works wherever you are in England, Scotland and Wales, and it goes straight to the company that runs your local power lines, not the one you pay your bill to. They'll tell you what's happened and roughly how long it'll be.
  4. Switch things off, except one light, so you know when it's back. When power returns, everything else coming on at once can trip it again.
  5. Keep the fridge and freezer shut. Every opening lets the cold out. More on that in Part 3.
Do it now, while the lights are on

Find your fuse box, and find your torch. Put 105 in your phone as a contact called "Power cut". It takes two minutes, and it's two minutes you won't have to spend in the dark.

Part 2

Who needs extra help

If anyone in your home relies on power for medical equipment, is older, has young children, or would struggle without heat or light, sign up to the Priority Services Register. It's free, run through your electricity network operator or your supplier. People on it get priority information during a cut, and in many cases practical help.

And think about who's next door. A quick knock on an older neighbour's door during a long cut is worth more than any kit list.

Part 3

Food: shut doors buy you time

UK food safety guidance is clear and reassuring:

  • A fridge keeps food safe for about 4 hours with the door shut.
  • A full freezer keeps food frozen for about 48 hours; a half-full one, about 24 hours. These vary with the appliance and the room.
  • Food stays safe while the fridge is below 8 °C. If it reaches 8 °C or more, eat chilled food within 4 hours, or throw it out.

A cheap fridge thermometer turns guessing into knowing. And a full freezer lasts twice as long as a half-empty one: bottles of water fill the gaps and become ice packs.

The order to eat things

  1. Chilled food from the fridge, first.
  2. Then defrosted food that doesn't need cooking.
  3. Then bread and other things that keep at room temperature.
  4. Tins, jars and dried food last. Tinned food is safe cold, straight from the tin.

Part 4

Light, warmth and cooking, without a fire

Power cuts don't usually hurt people. What people do to cope with them sometimes does. The fire services' advice comes down to three rules:

LightTorches and battery lanterns, not candles. If you must use a candle, a proper holder, away from curtains and anything that burns, and out before you leave the room.
CookingNever indoors on a barbecue, camping stove or patio heater, not even with the windows open. They make carbon monoxide: no smell, no colour, and deadly. Cook outside, in the open air.
WarmthLayers, blankets and hot drinks first. Gather everyone into one room and shut the door. Keep any portable heater at least a metre from people and furniture.

My own backup cooking is a small meths stove with a wind shield, used outdoors. I chose it partly because I'd rather not keep pressurised gas canisters in the house. Whatever you choose, try it once on a calm day, so the first time isn't in the dark and the rain.

Carbon monoxide alarm

Fit a battery CO alarm, and test it monthly. If it sounds: get everyone out into fresh air, then call for help. If anyone feels dizzy, sick or has a headache, call 999.

Part 5

If it goes on: the first night and after

  • Keep phones for calls. Lower the screen brightness, switch on power saving, and take turns with a charged power bank. (Living safely with lithium batteries has the power bank maths.)
  • Water. Mains water normally keeps flowing, but a few litres stored per person is cheap peace of mind.
  • A battery or wind-up radio keeps you informed when the internet and the mobile network struggle.
  • Routine and a job each. Meals at the usual times, a game, a book, a task for everyone. Boredom and worry are the real enemies of a long night.
  • When it comes back: switch things on one at a time, and check the freezer before you trust it.

Part 6

A weekend to get ready

Check

105 is in your phone, and you know where the fuse box is.

Check

A torch in a fixed place, with spare batteries, and one in each bedroom.

Check

A battery CO alarm fitted and tested.

Check

A fridge thermometer, and gaps in the freezer filled with bottles of water.

Check

A charged power bank, and a few days of food that needs no fridge.

Check

You've done a practice run: an evening with the lights, heating and screens off on purpose, and written down what you learned.

What I'd want you to take away

Ready, not scared

Know the number

105, free, straight to the people who fix it.

Shut doors

Four hours for the fridge, a day or two for the freezer, if you leave them alone.

No flames indoors

Torches for light, cooking outside, and a CO alarm on the wall.
