How networks work

The box your internet provider sent you is five devices squeezed into one piece of plastic. In part one you'll take it apart without a screwdriver, using commands on your own network. In part two you'll see how a business splits those same five jobs into separate kit, and why it's worth the cost.

Before you start

What you need

An Ubuntu computer on your home network, a terminal, and ideally a phone on the same Wi-Fi. Meet the terminal covers everything you'll need to type. Every command in part one only looks. Nothing here changes your network.

Only your own network

Explore networks you own or run. Poking around other people's, a café's or an employer's, can break their rules or the law, even when all you're doing is looking.

Part one

Your home network

Step 1

The box is five devices

It's usually called "the router", but the box from your provider does at least five separate jobs. In an office, each of these is often its own box.

ModemTalks to your provider over the phone line, fibre or cable. It's the door to the outside world.
RouterDecides where each piece of traffic goes: stay inside the house, or go out to the internet?
SwitchThe network sockets on the back. It passes traffic between wired devices in the house.
Wi-Fi access pointThe same as the switch, but by radio.
Address desk (DHCP)Hands every device an address when it joins, like a receptionist giving out visitor badges.

It usually does two more jobs as well: a basic firewall, and passing on your computer's questions about names like bbc.co.uk (DNS). Keep those in mind: they come back in steps 4 and 5.

Now follow one request, from your laptop to a website and back, through those jobs. Press Next, or tap any stop.

    Check

    You can name the five jobs the box does, and say which one gave your laptop its address.

    Step 2

    Find your own address, and the router's

    Every device on the network has an address, a set of four numbers. Ask your computer for its own, and for the way out:

    $ hostname -I        # my address on this network
$ ip route           # where does traffic go?

    In the ip route output, find the line starting default via. It reads something like default via 192.168.0.1 dev wlp2s0: "anything not on this network, send it to 192.168.0.1". That address is your router. The dev part is which connection it goes out through: names starting wl are Wi-Fi, en is a cable.

    Predict first

    Look at your address and the router's. What do you notice about the first three numbers? Why might that be?

    Why

    They usually match, for example 192.168.0.23 and 192.168.0.1. Addresses work a bit like postcodes: the first part says which network, the last part says which device on it. Everything at home shares the same "street", and the router is usually number 1. Addresses starting 192.168., 10., or 172.16. to 172.31. are private: they're reused inside millions of homes, and never appear on the internet itself.

    Check

    You know your computer's address, your router's address, and whether you're on Wi-Fi or a cable.

    Step 3

    Who else is here?

    Predict first

    Before you check, count: how many devices do you think are on your home network right now? Phones, TVs, speakers, the lot.

    $ ip neigh

    This lists the neighbours your computer has talked to recently: each one's address, and a hardware address (the lladdr, sometimes called a MAC address) that's built into its network chip. It only shows devices your computer has spoken to, so it undercounts.

    For the full list, ask the router. Type its address from step 2 into your browser, for example http://192.168.0.1, and log in with the password printed on the box or its card. Look for a page called something like Connected devices.

    What most people find

    More than they guessed, often twice as many. Smart TVs, a speaker, a doorbell, the printer, a phone someone forgot. Every one of them shares your network, which is why the Securing Ubuntu firewall matters even at home. If there's something you genuinely can't identify, change your Wi-Fi password: everything legitimate will have to rejoin, and whatever doesn't was never meant to be there.

    Check

    You've seen your router's list of connected devices, and you can say what most of them are.

    Step 4

    The address the world sees

    Predict first

    When you visit a website, it sees an address for you. Do you think it's the one hostname -I showed? Check: search for "what is my IP" in your browser.

    What's going on

    It's completely different. That's your public address: the one your provider gave the whole house. Every device inside shares it.

    The router does this with a trick called NAT, network address translation. As your request leaves, the router swaps your private address for the public one and keeps a note. When the reply comes back, it checks the note and passes it to the right device. It's like a company switchboard: one outside number, and the receptionist knows which extension asked.

    It's also why your home is fairly well protected by default. A stranger's request that arrives unasked matches no note, so the router has nowhere to send it and drops it.

    Check

    You've compared your private address with your public one, and you can explain why they're different.

    Step 5

    Follow a packet out

    Traffic travels in small pieces called packets. Two commands show the two halves of every visit to a website: first finding the address, then the journey there.

    $ resolvectl query bbc.co.uk    # name to address: that's DNS
$ tracepath -n bbc.co.uk        # every stop on the way

    tracepath prints one line per hop, each a router passing your packet on. It takes a little while. If it sits on no reply lines, press Ctrl + C: some routers simply don't answer.

    Predict first

    What do you think the very first hop will be?

    What you should see

    Your own router, the default via address from step 2. That's the only way out. Next come your provider's routers, often with a jump in the time column where the traffic enters its network, then the big internet routers, then the website. Each line's time is the round trip to that hop, in milliseconds.

    Check

    You've looked up a name with resolvectl, and seen your router as the first hop of a tracepath.

    Part two

    Scaling up to an office

    An office network does exactly the same jobs as the box at home. What changes is the numbers: a hundred people instead of four, hundreds of devices, visitors who need Wi-Fi but mustn't see the accounts, and a business that stops if the network does.

    Step 6

    Why a business splits the box

    Go back to the packet journey in step 1 and switch it to Office. The same request now passes through separate boxes, each doing one job and doing it properly:

    • A proper firewall, with rules written for the business: which services can reach what, and a record of what tried.
    • Managed switches, dozens of ports each, set up and monitored from one place. Usually one per floor or area (the "access" switches), all joined to a bigger one in the middle (the "core").
    • Many Wi-Fi access points in the ceilings, often powered through their network cable (called PoE, power over Ethernet), all managed together so people can walk the building without dropping off.
    • DHCP and DNS on servers, with room for thousands of devices and names for the office's own systems.
    • Structured cabling: every desk's socket wired back to a numbered patch panel in a comms cabinet, so any socket can be connected to anything without new cable.
    • No single point of failure where it matters: two internet lines from different providers, and a battery backup (a UPS) that keeps the network running through a power cut.
    Think about it

    The home box does all of this in one cheap piece of plastic. What's the price of that convenience?

    My answer

    When it breaks, everything breaks, all at once, and you can't upgrade one job without replacing the lot. At home, that's an annoying evening. In a business, it's a hundred people unable to work. Splitting the jobs costs money, but each piece can be chosen, upgraded, doubled up or fixed on its own.

    Check

    You've followed the office journey, and can say what each extra box does that the home one doesn't.

    Step 7

    One set of cables, several networks: VLANs

    An office doesn't want the visitors' Wi-Fi on the same network as the accounts computer, and it doesn't want to lay four sets of cables either. So managed switches split one physical network into several separate ones, called VLANs (virtual LANs). Each packet carries a small tag saying which VLAN it belongs to, and the switches keep them apart. A typical office might have:

    StaffEveryone's computers, and the systems they need.
    GuestsInternet only. Can't see anything else in the building.
    Phones and printersKept on their own, so a problem there doesn't spread.
    Cameras and building kitOften the least secure devices, so they're fenced off from everything.

    Traffic between VLANs has to pass through the firewall, which decides what's allowed. A guest can reach the internet but not the staff network, and it's all on the same cables.

    Experiment at home

    Many home routers have a guest Wi-Fi you can switch on in their settings: a small VLAN of your own. Run the test web server from Securing Ubuntu, question 3 on your computer, with its firewall switched off just for the test (sudo ufw disable, then sudo ufw enable afterwards). Try to reach it from your phone on your normal Wi-Fi, then on the guest network. What happens?

    What you should find

    On the normal Wi-Fi, the phone can reach it. On the guest Wi-Fi it usually can't, even though it's the same router and the same room. The guest network gets the internet and nothing else: exactly the guest VLAN from the table, in miniature. That's the idea behind most of part two. The home box already does it, just in one piece of plastic.

    Check

    You can explain what a VLAN is, and why guests and staff are kept apart even when they share the same cables.

    That's it

    What I'd want you to take away

    Same jobs, different scale

    Modem, router, switch, Wi-Fi, address desk. A castle or a cottage, the jobs don't change. Only how many boxes they're spread across.

    The first hop is always the router

    default via is the way out. When "the internet is down", the first question is whether you can even reach that.

    Separation is security

    NAT at home, VLANs in the office. Things that don't need to see each other shouldn't be able to.
