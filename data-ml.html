Part 1

Questions and answers

Machine learning has two words you'll see everywhere. The features are what you know about each penguin: its four measurements. The label is what you want it to work out: the species. By tradition, the features are called X (a table) and the label y (a single column).

Before any cleverness, ask what a lazy guess would score. If you just said "Adelie" every time, because that's the most common species, how often would you be right?

Lesson

44% is the score to beat. Any model has to do better than the laziest possible guess, or it's learnt nothing. Always work this number out first.

Part 2

Write the rule yourself

Look back at step 3's charts. Gentoos had the longest flippers, over about 206 mm. Of the rest, Chinstraps had the longer bills. That's a rule you could write on a card:

Experiment

Change the 44 to 42, then 46. Then try 45. Can you beat 315? How did you decide which way to move it?

What you just did

45 gets 316, one better; 42 and 46 are both worse. You just trained a model by hand: tried a setting, scored it, and nudged it. That loop of guess, score, adjust is exactly what scikit-learn does, only faster and over every measurement at once.

Part 3

Let the computer write the rule

A decision tree learns rules of exactly the kind you just wrote: a chain of "is this measurement bigger than that?" questions. You give it the questions and the answers, and it works out which questions to ask, and where to draw the lines. Every model in scikit-learn works the same three ways:

  • .fit(X, y): learn from these examples.
  • .predict(X): give your answers for these penguins.
  • .score(X, y): what fraction of these did you get right?
Predict first

The tree was allowed two questions. Which two measurements do you think it chose, and roughly where did it draw the lines?

Look familiar?

First flipper length, split at 206.5 mm. Then, for the shorter flippers, bill length, split at about 43.4 mm. That's your rule, found by the computer from nothing but the numbers. It scores a little higher, 96.4%, because it doesn't call every long-flippered penguin a Gentoo: it asks those a question too, about bill depth, and catches a few deep-billed Chinstraps your rule got wrong.

That's the whole idea of machine learning in one box. Nobody told it about flippers. It found the measurements that matter, the same ones you found by drawing them in step 3.

Part 4

A different idea: who do you look like?

k-nearest neighbours doesn't make rules at all. To name a penguin, it finds the 5 penguins whose measurements are closest to it, and goes with the majority. "You're probably the species of the penguins you most resemble." It's simple, and it often works very well.

Think about it

83.8%. That's worse than your two-line rule. Something is wrong. Here's a clue: to measure "closest", it adds up the differences in all four measurements. Look at X.head(3) again. What's different about one of the columns?

The trap

Weight is in grams. Two penguins can differ by 500 in body mass but only 5 in bill length. To the model, the 500 is a hundred times bigger, so "closest" ends up meaning "closest in weight", and weight was the measurement that kept Adelies and Chinstraps mixed up in step 3. The other three columns hardly count.

The model didn't know the units, and it doesn't care. It saw big numbers and small numbers.

The fix is to rescale every column so they're all on the same footing before measuring distance. StandardScaler does this: for each column it subtracts the average and divides by the spread, so a typical difference is about 1 in every column. A pipeline glues the scaling and the model together so they always happen in order:

Lesson

Same data, same model, one line of scaling: from 84% to 99%. Models that measure distance need every column on the same scale. Ask "what are the units?" before you trust any score.

Experiment

Try a half-fix instead: go back to the plain knn box and give it X with the weight in kilograms (X2 = X.copy(), then X2["body_mass_g"] = X2["body_mass_g"] / 1000). Where does the score land, and why isn't it quite as good?

Why kilograms isn't enough

About 97.9%: much better, still short of 99.1%. Now flipper length is the bully. Flippers vary over about 60 mm, bill depth over about 8 mm, so flippers still shout loudest. Changing the units fixes one column by luck. Scaling fixes all of them on purpose.

And a penguin that couldn't exist

Try a bill of 300 mm and a mass of 50,000 g. The model will still answer, and still sound sure. A model always gives an answer. It has no way to say "that's not a penguin". Spotting nonsense is still your job.

Part 6

Where does it go wrong?

A single score hides which penguins it gets wrong. A crosstab of truth against guesses, called a confusion matrix, shows it. Rows are what each penguin really is; columns are what the model said. Start with your own rule:

Everything on the diagonal is right. Your rule's mistakes are mostly Adelies and Chinstraps swapped: the pair that sat on top of each other in step 3. Now the scaled model, and a picture of the penguins it got wrong:

Just three, all Chinstraps it called Adelie. Two sit right at the edge of the Adelie patch, where an expert might have hesitated too. The third looks like a Chinstrap on this chart, but the model was also weighing flipper length and body mass, which this chart doesn't show. A chart shows two measurements; the model uses all four. Part 7

Your turn: fewer clues

Challenge

Does the model need all four measurements? Make a new X with just two columns, say clean[["bill_length_mm", "flipper_length_mm"]], and train the scaled model on it. Try a few pairs. Which pair keeps the score highest? Which pair is worst?

One way to do it 
X_two = clean[["bill_length_mm", "flipper_length_mm"]]
two = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=5))
two.fit(X_two, y)
two.score(X_two, y)

Every pair with bill length does well, about 96% to 97%. Every pair without it lands around 84%, with flipper and body mass the worst of all. That's step 3's finding in numbers: bill length is the one measurement that tells Chinstraps from Adelies. Remember to fit a fresh model each time: two is new, so it doesn't overwrite model.

The catch

99%. But who marked the homework?

Every score on this page came from asking the model about the same penguins it learnt from. That's like a teacher handing out the answers, then setting the same questions in the exam. Nearest neighbours is especially lucky: when it looks for the 5 penguins closest to one it already knows, the closest of all is that very penguin.

Think about it

How would you find out how well it does on penguins it has never seen, when all you have are these 333?

What I'd want you to take away

Beat the lazy guess

44% for always saying Adelie. Work out the baseline first, so you know what a score is worth.

Units matter to a model

Grams swamped millimetres until every column was rescaled. Same model, 84% to 99%.

Fit, predict, score

Every scikit-learn model works the same way, so the next one you meet already looks familiar.
