The 555 jumper bench

When I got back into electronics, the first chip I reached for was the 555 timer. It's from 1971, it costs pennies, and it's still in everything that blinks or beeps. I always wanted a kit where you could move a jumper to swap a resistor or a capacitor and see what changed. So I built one, on this page. Move the jumpers, watch the result on an oscilloscope, listen to it, then build the real thing.

Before you start

Where this picks up

This guide assumes you've done part two. The most important bit is the capacitor: it fills like a bucket, and resistance × capacitance tells you how long that takes. The 555 is built on exactly that idea. It just keeps filling and emptying the bucket, over and over, and flips its output each time.

Same safety rule

Batteries only. Nothing in this guide ever plugs into the wall.

The 555 is happy on anything from about 4.5 V to 15 V, so a 9 V battery is ideal. Electrolytic capacitors still have a right way round: the stripe goes to the negative side.

Stage 1

A bucket with two tripwires

Inside the 555 are two tripwires that watch the voltage on the capacitor, C1:

  1. While the output is on, C1 fills from the battery through R1 and R2.
  2. When C1 reaches two thirds of the battery voltage, the top tripwire fires. The output switches off, and pin 7 opens a drain to the negative side.
  3. C1 empties through that drain, but only through R2.
  4. When C1 falls to one third, the bottom tripwire fires. The drain closes, the output switches back on, and C1 starts filling again.

Round and round, for as long as the battery lasts. A circuit that keeps flipping by itself like this is called an astable: it never settles in one state.

Here's the bench. Each red cap is a jumper: click a pair of pins to move it, and that part goes into the circuit. The oscilloscope shows two things at once: the amber line is the output on pin 3, and the green line is the voltage on C1. The dashed lines are the two tripwires.

Predict, then try

The bench starts on the LED flasher: R1 10k, R2 47k, C1 10 µF. Before you touch anything, guess: if you move C1's jumper to 1 µF, a tenth of the size, what happens to the flashing?

What happens

It flashes ten times faster, about 14 times a second instead of 1.4. That's too fast for your eye to follow, so the LED seems to glow steadily instead. A bucket a tenth the size fills and empties in a tenth of the time. Every time in a 555 is resistance × capacitance, just like the fading LED in part two.

Spot the long first pulse

Look at the very start of the trace. The first time the output is on, it stays on longer than every later time. That's because, at switch-on, C1 is empty. It has to fill all the way from nothing up to two thirds, rather than from one third. After that, it only ever travels between the two tripwires. A real oscilloscope shows exactly the same thing.

Checkpoint

Can you explain:

  • what the two tripwires do, and at what voltages,
  • why making C1 ten times smaller makes it flash ten times faster?

Stage 2

Turning the dials

Now find out what each part does. Set each row on the bench, read the frequency and the duty cycle, and fill in the table on paper. The duty cycle is how much of each cycle the output spends on: 50% means on and off for equal times.

Predict, then try

Change the battery from 5 V to 9 V to 12 V. A bigger battery pushes charge in harder. Will the 555 run faster?

What you've found

The frequency doesn't change at all. A bigger battery does fill C1 faster, but the tripwires move up with it, because they're set at a third and two thirds of whatever the battery is. The faster filling and the higher target cancel out exactly. That's one of the reasons the 555 became so popular: its timing doesn't drift as a battery runs down.

Predict, then try

Set R1 and R2 to the same value, say both 10 kΩ. Can you get a duty cycle of exactly 50%?

What you've found

No: it sits at about 67%. Look back at stage 1. C1 fills through R1 and R2 together, but empties through R2 alone. Filling always meets more resistance than emptying, so the output is always on for longer than it's off. The fourth row of the table shows how bad it gets: with a big R1 and a small R2, it's on almost all the time.

The fix is the diode. Tick D1 on the bench. A diode lets current through one way only, so while C1 is filling, the current takes the shortcut through the diode and skips R2. Now filling uses R1 only and emptying uses R2 only. Make them equal, and you get an even 50% square wave. That's the Even square wave setting.

The sums, if you want them

The readings on the bench come from three small formulas. The 0.693 is how long a capacitor takes to fill or empty between a third and two thirds, measured in units of resistance × capacitance.

Stage 3

Build it for real

Time to build the LED flasher on the breadboard: R1 10k, R2 47k, C1 10 µF. The bench says it should flash about 1.4 times a second.

Which pin is which

The 555 has 8 legs, 4 down each side. Find the notch or the dot at one end. With that end pointing away from you, pin 1 is top left, and the numbers go down the left side and back up the right, anticlockwise.

  1. Push the 555 into the breadboard across the middle gap, so each leg has its own row.
  2. Pin 8 and pin 4 to the red line. Pin 4 is reset: tied to positive, it never resets.
  3. Pin 1 to the blue line.
  4. R1, 10 kΩ, from the red line to pin 7.
  5. R2, 47 kΩ, from pin 7 to pin 6.
  6. A jumper wire from pin 6 to pin 2. Both tripwires watch the same capacitor.
  7. C1, 10 µF, from pin 2 to the blue line, stripe to the blue line.
  8. From pin 3: a 470 Ω resistor, then the LED (long leg first), to the blue line.
  9. Connect the battery.
Keep R1 at 1 kΩ or more

Every time C1 empties, pin 7 also carries the current coming down through R1 from the battery. With R1 tiny, that current gets big enough to cook the chip. The bench warns you when you get close.

Measure it

Count the flashes for 10 seconds. The bench says about 14. What do you get?

Why yours is different

You'll probably count anything from about 11 to 17, and that's not a mistake. The bench uses perfect parts. Electrolytic capacitors are made loosely: a "10 µF" one can easily be 20% bigger or smaller than its label, and resistors are only within 5%. Real parts are close, not exact. The gap between what the sums predict and what the bench in front of you actually does is where most of the learning in electronics happens.

If you get the chance to put a real oscilloscope on pins 3 and 6, you'll see the same two traces as the bench, with a few extra details the simulation leaves out, like a small spike each time the output switches.

Now swap parts the way the jumpers did. Try C1 at 1 µF, then put a 100 nF capacitor in and the speaker on pin 3 in place of the LED. You've built a beeper. Put the 10 kΩ trimmer in series with R2, and you can tune it.

Checkpoint

Can you explain:

  • which pin is pin 1, and how to count the rest,
  • why your real flash count isn't exactly the bench's?

What I'd want you to take away

Two tripwires

The 555 fills and empties a capacitor between a third and two thirds of the battery, and flips its output each time.

R × C sets the time

Charge through R1 and R2, empty through R2. Bigger parts, slower timing. The battery voltage cancels out.

Real parts aren't exact

The sums get you close. The chip in front of you will be a little off, and finding out why is the fun part.
