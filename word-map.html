Where does “dog” live?

The counting machine on the last page knew that "cat" and "dog" were different marks, and nothing else. So everything it learned about one was useless for the other. On this page you'll watch a machine fix that by giving every word a place on a map, and learning where to put it. Then you'll teach it a brand-new animal from one sentence, and watch it guess things about that animal it was never told.

Before you start

What you need

A web browser. Everything here runs on your own device, and nothing you do is sent anywhere. It helps to have done Guess the next word first, because this page picks up exactly where that one stopped. And if you've watched S.M.A.R.T. learn, you've already seen the trick this page uses: guess, check, nudge, repeat.

When I built my own language model from scratch, this was the step where it stopped being a lookup table and started being something that learns.

Step 1

A fox walks into the tiny world

Here's the same tiny world as last time: thirty sentences about a cat, a dog, a mouse and a bird. Now add one more sentence, about an animal it has never met:

the fox sat on the mat.

Predict first

You've read one sentence about a fox. What else do you think a fox might do in this world? And what would the counting machine say comes after "fox"?

What the counting machine says

It has seen "fox" exactly once, followed by "sat". So its table row for "fox" says: sat, 100%. Nothing else is even possible. You, on the other hand, would guess a fox might chase things, eat things, sleep somewhere, because it's an animal, and that's what animals do in this world.

You can make that guess because, in your head, "fox" sits near "cat" and "dog". The counting machine has no "near". Every word is just a different mark, equally unlike every other.

Check

You can say why the counting machine can't guess that a fox might chase or eat something.

Step 2

Give every word a place

The fix is to give the machine a sense of "near". We give every word two numbers, and treat them like a grid reference on a map: how far across, how far up. Words with similar numbers sit close together.

To start with, nobody knows where any word should go, so every word gets a random place. Here's the map. Every dot is a word from the tiny world:

Step 3

Let it learn where words belong

Now the machine plays the same game as last time: guess the next word. But instead of looking up a table, it guesses using the place of the word it's looking at. Each part of the map leans towards certain next words.

It goes through every pair of words in the tiny world, guesses, and checks the answer. Where it was wrong, it nudges the word a little towards a place that would have guessed better. Then it does it all again. That's one round. Press Learn on the map above and it runs four thousand rounds while you watch.

Keep an eye on "How surprised it is". It's a measure of how badly the machine's guesses missed, on average. At the random start it's about 3.4, which is what you'd score by guessing any word at random. Lower is better. It never gets near zero, because even you can't know whether "the cat" will be followed by "sat" or "chased".

Predict first

Once it settles, look at who ended up together. Did your guess from step 2 come true?

What it grouped, and why it's odd

The animals end up near each other. But look at the big pile: chased, ate, watched, found, climbed, and also on, under, over, by and after, nearly on top of each other. A verb and a preposition aren't alike at all to you. So why?

Because every one of them is followed by "the". This machine only ever learned to guess the next word, so it put words together when what comes after them is the same. The things that end a sentence (mat, rug, wall, bone) form another pile, because they're all followed by a full stop.

It doesn't know what any word means. It has learned what job each word does. That turns out to be enough to be useful, as the next step shows.

Try Start again and learn a few more times. Each start is random, so the map comes out rotated or flipped. But the same groups form every time, which tells you they're real, not luck.

Check

You've watched the surprise fall, and can explain why "chased" ends up next to "on".

What happened

The counting table still says sat, 100%. The word map says sat is the most likely, but it also gives real chances to found, ran, ate, slept and chased. It was never shown a fox doing any of those.

Look at the map: the fox has landed among the animals. To get "sat" right, the best place for the fox was near the other words that are followed by "sat", and those are the animals. Once it's there, it inherits everything that part of the map has learned about animals. One sentence was enough.

That's the cat-and-dog problem from the last page, solved. What the machine learns about one word now carries over to the words near it.

Check

You've added the fox, compared the two answers, and can say where the fox's extra guesses came from.

Step 5

From two numbers to the real thing

What you've built has a proper name. The numbers for each word are its embedding, and every AI chatbot you've heard of starts here: the first thing it does with your words is turn each one into its list of learned numbers. The real ones differ from this page in three ways.

  • Far more numbers. Two numbers make a flat map with room for very little. Look at how crowded yours is: mat and rug sit on top of each other. Real models give every word hundreds of numbers, and the biggest give it thousands. You can't draw that as a map, but "near" still works exactly the same way.
  • Pieces of words, not whole words. Real models cut text into common chunks, so a word they've never seen can still be built from pieces they know.
  • Far more reading. The same nudging, but over more text than a person could read in many lifetimes.

But notice what hasn't changed. This machine still looks back exactly one word. It knows a fox is like a cat, but in "the fox that the dog chased ran under the …" it has no idea which animal is running. Looking a long way back, and working out which earlier words matter to the next one, is the other half of a transformer. It's called attention, and it's the next page on this path.

Check

You know what an embedding is, why real ones have far more than two numbers, and what this machine still can't do.

That's it

What I'd want you to take away

A word is a place

Each word becomes a few numbers, learned, not chosen. Similar numbers mean the machine treats the words alike.

It learns jobs, not meanings

Words land together when they do the same job. "Chased" sits by "on" because both are followed by "the".

Near means shared

What it learns about one word carries over to its neighbours. That's how one sentence taught it about a fox.
