Browse like you mean it

Your browser is the door between you and every website you visit. Most of its security comes switched on, but a few settings are worth choosing on purpose, and a few habits matter more than any setting. This guide covers eight, for Chrome, Firefox, Edge and Safari. It starts with a live look at what this very page can see about you. Choose your browser and the steps change to match.

Working it out…
This page saves none of it and sends none of it. Like every website, though, the server that delivers it does see your IP address, which tells it roughly where you are.
Think about it

None of those facts is secret on its own. Lots of people have the same screen, the same language, the same time zone. So how could a website use them to recognise you?

The answer

By combining them. Your exact mix of browser version, system, screen, language, time zone, fonts and graphics card can be unusual enough to pick you out of a crowd, with no cookie needed. That's called fingerprinting. The tracker settings in Check 4 make your browser look more like everyone else's.

Using Brave, Opera, Vivaldi or Samsung Internet? They're built on the same engine as Chrome, so the Chrome steps mostly apply, with slightly different menu names.

Check 1

Up to date

Browsers are attacked more than almost any other program, and they're fixed constantly. An out-of-date browser is the easiest way in.

Menu ⋮ › Help › About Google Chrome. It checks and updates. Press Relaunch when asked: the fix isn't in until you do.

Menu ☰ › Help › About Firefox. It downloads updates and asks you to Restart to update.

Menu … › Help and feedback › About Microsoft Edge. Restart when it's done.

Safari updates with the system. On a Mac: System Settings › General › Software Update. On an iPhone or iPad: Settings › General › Software Update.

Lesson

An update isn't installed until the browser restarts. If yours has been open for weeks, it may be running old code.

Check 2

Secure connections only

The padlock and https mean the connection is encrypted, so nobody on the same Wi-Fi can read or change what's sent. Tell your browser to always try for that, and to warn you before it falls back to an unencrypted page.

Settings › Privacy and security › Security. Turn on Always use secure connections.

Settings › Privacy & Security, scroll to HTTPS-Only Mode, and choose Enable HTTPS-Only Mode in all windows.

Settings › Privacy, search, and services, then the Security section. Turn on the option to switch to more secure connections (the exact wording changes between versions; search Settings for "secure connections").

Safari upgrades to secure connections and warns about insecure pages by default. On an iPhone, check that Not Secure Connection Warning is on in Safari's settings (Settings › Apps › Safari, or Settings › Safari on older versions).

Check 3

Warnings about dangerous sites

Every main browser can warn you before you open a known scam or malware site. It should be on. In some browsers you can choose how much checking it does.

Settings › Privacy and security › Security: Safe Browsing. Standard protection is on by default. Enhanced protection checks more and reacts faster, but sends more of what you browse to Google to do it. That's a trade-off worth choosing on purpose. Never pick "No protection".

Settings › Privacy & Security, under Deceptive Content and Dangerous Software Protection: keep Block dangerous and deceptive content ticked.

Settings › Privacy, search, and services › Security: keep Microsoft Defender SmartScreen on, and the option to block potentially unwanted apps.

Keep Fraudulent Website Warning on: on a Mac, Safari › Settings › Security; on an iPhone, in Safari's settings.

Check 4

Trackers and fingerprinting

Trackers follow you from site to site, building up a picture of what you read and buy. Every main browser now blocks some of them. You can usually turn it up.

Settings › Privacy and security › Third-party cookies: choose Block third-party cookies. Most sites still work. If one breaks, you can allow it for that site alone.

Settings › Privacy & Security, Enhanced Tracking Protection. Standard is good. Strict blocks more, including fingerprinters and all cross-site cookies, and can occasionally break a site: the shield in the address bar turns it off for that site.

Settings › Privacy, search, and services, Tracking prevention. Balanced is the default; Strict blocks the most, but may break some site features.

Keep Prevent Cross-Site Tracking on, and set Advanced Tracking and Fingerprinting Protection to cover all browsing, not just private windows. Both are in Safari's settings (on a Mac, under Safari › Settings › Privacy and Advanced).

Then come back to this page and look at the panel again. Some of the details may now read differently or vaguer. That's the protection working.

Check 5

Private lookups: secure DNS

Before your browser opens a site, it asks "where is this name?" Normally that question goes out unencrypted, so your internet provider, or anyone on the same Wi-Fi, can see every site you look up. Secure DNS encrypts the question.

Settings › Privacy and security › Security: turn on Use secure DNS, and choose a provider, or keep your current one if it supports it.

Settings › Privacy & Security, DNS over HTTPS: choose Increased Protection or Max Protection.

Settings › Privacy, search, and services › Security: turn on Use secure DNS, and choose a provider.

Safari uses the system's settings rather than its own. Secure DNS on Apple devices is set up for the whole device or the home network, which is beyond this guide. The other checks still apply.

Whichever provider you pick now sees your lookups instead. Choose one whose privacy promises you're happy with.

Check 6

Extensions: the ones you forgot

Some extensions can read and change everything on every website you visit, including your bank. Extensions get sold, and sometimes the new owner turns them bad overnight. Keep only the ones you use, from makers you trust.

Type chrome://extensions in the address bar. For each one: do I still use it? Click Details to see its permissions. Remove what you don't need.

Type about:addons in the address bar. Review each extension and its permissions, and remove what you don't need.

Type edge://extensions in the address bar. Review each one, and remove what you don't need.

On a Mac: Safari › Settings › Extensions. On an iPhone: Settings › Apps › Safari › Extensions. Turn off or delete what you don't use.

Predict first

How many extensions do you think you have? Check. Most people find at least one they don't remember installing.

Check 7

Notifications and site permissions

"Allow notifications?" A lot of scam pop-ups that look like virus warnings are just a website you once clicked "Allow" on. Review which sites can notify you, use your location, camera or microphone, and remove the ones you don't want.

Settings › Privacy and security › Site settings, then Notifications, Location, Camera and Microphone. Consider Don't allow sites to send notifications as your default.

Settings › Privacy & Security, the Permissions section. Click Settings… next to Notifications, Location, Camera and Microphone. You can tick Block new requests for each.

Settings › Cookies and site permissions, then Notifications, Location, Camera and Microphone.

On a Mac: Safari › Settings › Websites, then each permission down the left. On an iPhone, websites can only send notifications once added to the Home Screen; manage those in Settings › Notifications.

Check

The panel at the top shows whether this page has notification permission. It should say it hasn't asked, and it never will.

Check 8

Passwords

A browser's built-in password manager is far better than reusing passwords, as long as your computer or phone itself is locked with a password or PIN. A separate password manager is also a good choice. Either way:

  • Use a different password for every site, and let the manager make them up.
  • Run the breach check, which tells you if any saved password has turned up in a known data breach: in Chrome, the Checkup in Google Password Manager; in Firefox, the breach alerts in your saved passwords; in Edge, Password Monitor in the password settings; for Safari, the security recommendations in Apple's Passwords app.
  • Never save passwords in a browser on a shared or public computer.

For the bigger job of sorting out every account you have, see Who holds the keys to your life?

Habits beat settings

Four things no setting does for you

  • Type the address, or use a bookmark, for your bank and email. Don't follow links in messages to them.
  • A padlock isn't a promise. It means the connection is private, not that the site is honest. Scam sites have padlocks too.
  • Close the "virus found!" page, don't call the number on it. Real browsers and real antivirus never ask you to ring anyone.
  • Download from the maker, not from a "free download" site. Is it the real thing? covers why.

What I'd want you to take away

The door, kept locked

Restart to update

A browser fix isn't in until the browser restarts.

Fewer trackers, fewer extensions

Turn tracker blocking up, and keep only extensions you use from makers you trust.

Habits first

Bookmark your bank, distrust pop-ups, and remember a padlock means private, not honest.
