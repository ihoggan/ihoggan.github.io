Electronics: stripes, storage and switches

Part one left you with three parts you know well: a battery, a resistor and an LED. This part opens the rest of the bag. You'll read a resistor's stripes without a meter, make an LED fade out slowly after you've switched it off, and use a transistor to let a tiny current control a bigger one. That last one is the part every computer is made of.

Before you start

Where this picks up

This guide assumes you've done part one: you can light an LED with a resistor, use a multimeter, and you've met Ohm's law, V = I × R. It works the same way. Each stage has a circuit on the page you can try before buying anything, and ends with a checkpoint: questions you should be able to answer in your own words.

Same safety rule, plus one

Batteries only. Nothing in this guide ever plugs into the wall.

New for this part: the capacitors here have a right way round, and a voltage limit printed on the side. Fitted backwards, or used above that limit, they can swell up or pop. Stage 2 shows you how to read both before you plug one in.

Stage 1

Reading the stripes

Resistors are too small to print a number on, so their value is painted on as coloured stripes. Once you know the code, you can pick the right one from a jumbled bag in seconds, no meter needed.

Most resistors have four stripes. Hold it with the gold or silver stripe on the right. Then, reading from the left:

  1. First stripe: the first digit.
  2. Second stripe: the second digit.
  3. Third stripe: how many noughts to add after them.
  4. Fourth stripe: how accurate it is. Gold means within 5%.

Each colour stands for a number, in the order of a rainbow with black and brown at the start and grey and white at the end:

0 black1 brown2 red3 orange4 yellow5 green6 blue7 violet8 grey9 white

So yellow, violet, brown means 4, 7, then one nought: 470 Ω, the resistor from part one.

Try this

Set the stripes for the other resistors from part one: 1 kΩ (1,000 Ω), 2.2 kΩ and 10 kΩ. Then press Test me until you can read five in a row without looking at the colour list.

The answers, and a shortcut

1 kΩ is brown, black, red. 2.2 kΩ is red, red, red. 10 kΩ is brown, black, orange. The shortcut: the third stripe tells you the size at a glance. Brown means hundreds of ohms, red means thousands, orange means tens of thousands.

And when the stripes are faded or you can't tell red from orange under your lamp, which happens, fall back on the meter, set to Ω, exactly as in part one.

Checkpoint

Can you:

  • say which way round to hold a resistor to read it,
  • read a four-stripe resistor's value from its colours?

Stage 2

Capacitors: a bucket for charge

A capacitor stores a little electricity and gives it back. In the water picture from part one, it's a small bucket: connect it to the battery and it fills up, disconnect the battery and whatever is attached can drain it. How much it holds is measured in farads. A whole farad is huge, so you'll see microfarads, millionths of a farad, written µF.

Here's a circuit that shows it off. The capacitor sits beside the LED. While the switch is on, the battery fills the capacitor and lights the LED. Then switch it off.

Switch it on, wait for the capacitor to fill, then switch it off.

The model is simplified, like part one's. A real LED fades a little more gradually at the very end, and real capacitors are only accurate to within about 20%. Expect your timings to differ by that much.

Predict first

In part one, switching off made the LED go out instantly, because the loop was broken. Here, the switch breaks the loop to the battery. What do you expect the LED to do?

What happens

It keeps glowing, then fades away. The battery is cut off, but the capacitor is full, and it's still connected to the LED. It empties itself through the LED, and as it empties, its push gets weaker, so the light dims. That small bucket is exactly how a gadget keeps its clock running for a moment while you swap the batteries.

Look at the numbers

What happens to the glow time when the capacitor roughly doubles? Now keep 1000 µF and try the other resistors. What does the resistor do to it?

What you've found

Double the capacitor, and the glow lasts about twice as long: a bigger bucket takes longer to empty. A bigger resistor makes it last longer too, because it narrows the pipe the bucket drains through, though the LED starts dimmer.

Engineers wrap this up as the time constant: resistance × capacitance. In ohms and farads, the answer comes out in seconds. 1 kΩ × 1000 µF is 1,000 × 0.001, which is 1 second. In that time the capacitor does about two-thirds of its emptying (or filling), and after about five of them it's as good as finished. It's the same rule whether it's timing a blinking light or smoothing out a power supply.

With real parts

An electrolytic capacitor is a little can with two legs. Two things to read before you use one:

  • Which way round. The longer leg is positive. The side of the can has a stripe, usually with minus signs on it: that leg is negative. Positive goes towards the battery's +.
  • The voltage. It's printed on the can, like 25V. That's the most it can take. With a 9 V battery, anything 16 V or above is fine.
  1. Build part one's LED circuit, but use a 1 kΩ resistor.
  2. Fit a 1000 µF capacitor straight across the two edge lines: its positive leg in the red line, its negative leg in the blue line. That puts it across the LED and its resistor together.
  3. Use a push button between the battery's red wire and the red line, as your switch.
  4. Hold the button for a couple of seconds, then let go, and count.
Checkpoint

Can you explain:

  • why the LED keeps glowing after the battery is cut off,
  • what makes the glow last longer, and how to spot a capacitor's positive leg?

Stage 3

The transistor: a switch with no moving parts

A transistor has three legs, and one job worth learning first: a small current into one leg controls a much bigger current through the other two. The legs are called the base (the control), the collector and the emitter (the path the big current takes).

Here, the LED and its resistor sit on the big path. The only thing you can touch is a much smaller loop: a switch and a resistor going into the base.

Meters

Into the base (the small loop)
0 µA
Through the LED (the big loop)
0.0 mA
Big current ÷ small current
–

The model assumes a transistor that multiplies by 200. Real BC547s vary a lot, from roughly 100 to 800, even within one bag. The behaviour is the same; only the exact numbers move.

Predict first

Switch it on with the 10 kΩ base resistor. Before you look at the meters: which current do you think is bigger, the one into the base, or the one through the LED?

What the meters say

The LED's current is many times bigger: under a milliamp in the small loop is switching around fifteen milliamps through the LED. You're only moving the small one. The transistor lets the battery do the heavy lifting.

Look at the numbers

With 10 kΩ and 100 kΩ the LED current is the same, even though the base current is ten times smaller with 100 kΩ. Why do you think it stops at that level?

What you've found

Below a certain point, the LED's current is the base current multiplied, about 200 times for this transistor. That's what the 1 MΩ and 10 MΩ rows show: a tenth of the base current gives a tenth of the LED current.

But the multiplied current can't be more than the big loop allows. The 470 Ω resistor and the LED limit it to about 15 mA, using Ohm's law from part one. Once the base current is big enough to ask for more than that, the transistor is fully on, and giving it more changes nothing. That's using a transistor as a switch: give the base plenty, and it's either fully on or fully off. That's why 10 kΩ is the usual choice: it keeps the transistor firmly on, whatever the gain of the one you happen to pick out of the bag.

A computer chip is billions of transistors used exactly like this, each one switching the others on and off.

With real parts

A BC547 is a little black half-cylinder with three legs. Hold it with the flat face towards you and the legs pointing down. From left to right, the legs are collector, base, emitter.

Check the leg order

Other transistors in the same-shaped case, like the 2N2222, have their legs in a different order. If you use anything other than a BC547, look up its pinout before you wire it.

  1. Push the transistor into the breadboard so each leg has its own row.
  2. From the red line: a 470 Ω resistor, then the LED (long leg first), into the collector's row.
  3. A jumper wire from the emitter's row to the blue line.
  4. A 10 kΩ resistor from the base's row to a push button, and the other side of the button to the red line.
  5. Connect the battery, and press the button.

If you're curious, take the button and 10 kΩ resistor out, and instead touch one fingertip to the red line and another to the base's row. Your skin is the base resistor now. Depending on how dry your fingers are, the LED may glow faintly, or not at all. 9 V through your fingers is perfectly safe.

Checkpoint

Can you explain:

  • what "a small current controls a big one" means, using the meters,
  • why a 10 kΩ base resistor and a 100 kΩ one both light the LED fully?

Where next

Hand the switch to a computer

You've now got a switch that a tiny current can work. That matters, because the pins on a Raspberry Pi can only give a tiny current: enough for one LED, not much more. A transistor in between lets a Pi switch something bigger, like a motor or a strip of lights. Hand the switch to a computer is the next guide on this path.
