Meet the terminal

In films the terminal is where hackers work. In real life it's just typing instructions instead of clicking them. This page opens it, teaches you to read what it's telling you, and walks you round your own files. Every command here only looks at things, so nothing you do on this page can break your computer.

Before you start

What you need, and the one rule

A computer running Ubuntu Linux. Mine is a 2015 desktop that was headed for the scrap pile. Any recent version of Ubuntu will match what you see here.

No Ubuntu yet, or reading this on your phone? Use the practice terminal just below. It understands every command on this page, and it runs entirely in your browser.

The one rule

Never run a command you've copied from the internet unless you understand what it does. That includes this page. Every command here is explained before you're asked to type it. If a guide ever asks you to paste something long starting with sudo without saying why, stop.

Step 1

Open the terminal

The quickest way is a keyboard shortcut. Hold down Ctrl and Alt, then press T.

If that does nothing, click Show Apps, the grid of dots at the bottom of the bar down the left of the screen, and type terminal. Click the icon that appears.

You'll use it a lot, so keep it handy: right-click its icon in the bar on the left and choose Pin to Dash.

Check

A window is open with one line of text ending in $, and a flashing block after it. That line is called the prompt: the computer is waiting for you.

Step 2

Read what the prompt is telling you

The prompt isn't decoration. It answers three questions every time: who you are, which computer you're typing into, and where you are on it. Here's mine. Tap each part.

I called my computer nix6 when I installed Ubuntu, so that's what my prompt shows, and you'll see it in my other guides. Yours says whatever name was chosen for yours when Ubuntu was installed.

Predict first

Before you type anything: what do you think your prompt says your username and computer name are? Say it out loud, then check with these two commands. Type each one and press Enter.

$ whoami
$ hostname

whoami prints your username. hostname prints the computer's name. Commands are short and lower case because people have been typing them since the 1970s.

Check

The two answers match the two names in your prompt, before and after the @.

Step 3

Find out where you are, and move around

The terminal is always "in" one folder, just like a file window is always showing one. The ~ in your prompt means your home folder, where your own files live. Three commands do almost all the moving:

$ pwd          # print working directory: where am I?
$ ls           # list: what's in here?
$ cd Documents # change directory: go into Documents

Anything after a # is a comment for you to read. You don't type it. ("Directory" is the older word for a folder. They mean the same thing.)

Predict first

After cd Documents, the prompt will change. What do you think it'll say?

Show me

It becomes iain@nix6:~/Documents$. The ~ part grew into ~/Documents, meaning "the Documents folder inside my home folder". Now type cd .. (two dots) to go back up one level, and cd on its own to jump straight home from anywhere.

Capitals matter. cd documents fails because the folder is called Documents. That trips everyone up at least once.

Check

You went into Documents, watched the prompt change, and came back home with cd. The prompt shows just ~ again.

Step 4

Let the computer do the typing

Nobody types long names in full. Two keys save you most of the effort, and most of the typos:

  • Tab finishes a name for you. Type cd Doc and press Tab: it fills in Documents/.
  • ↑ brings back the last command you ran. Press it again to go further back.
Experiment

Type cd D and press Tab. Nothing happens. Why not? Press Tab a second time and look at what appears.

What was going on

Three folders start with D: Desktop, Documents and Downloads. The terminal won't guess, so the first Tab does nothing. The second Tab lists the choices. Type one more letter, o, and it's down to two. Add c and there's only one left, so Tab finishes it.

Check

You've finished a folder name with Tab, and brought back an old command with ↑.

Step 5

Four Windows habits that will catch you out

These are the moments when people coming from Windows decide the terminal is broken and close it. It isn't broken. It just has different habits.

Ctrl + C doesn't copy

In the terminal, Ctrl + C means stop. To feel why that matters, run a command that never finishes on its own. ping checks whether another computer answers, once a second, forever:

$ ping bbc.co.uk

Watch a few replies arrive, then press Ctrl + C. It stops, and prints a summary.

Copy and paste need Shift

Copy is Ctrl + Shift + C, and paste is Ctrl + Shift + V. Right-click also gives you Copy and Paste.

Passwords are invisible

When a command asks for your password, nothing appears as you type. Not even dots. It is working. Type the password and press Enter. If you get it wrong, it simply asks again.

No news is good news

Many commands print nothing at all when they succeed. cd Documents just gives you a new prompt. If something goes wrong, you'll get a message saying so.

Check

You started ping and stopped it with Ctrl + C, and the prompt came back.

Step 6

Ask the terminal for help

Every command comes with its own instructions. There are two ways to read them:

$ ls --help     # a quick summary, printed straight out
$ man ls        # the full manual page

The manual fills the window and waits for you to read it. Scroll with the arrow keys or the space bar, and press Q to get out. That last part isn't written anywhere on the screen, which is why it catches everyone.

Experiment

Skim ls --help for an option that shows hidden files. Run ls with it. What appeared that wasn't there before, and what do the new names have in common?

What you should find

It's ls -a, for "all". The extra names all start with a dot, like .bashrc. On Linux, a name starting with a dot is hidden. These are mostly settings files, which is why they're kept out of the way.

Check

You opened man ls, got out again with Q, and found the hidden files with ls -a.

That's it

What I'd want you to take away

Read the prompt

It tells you who you are, which computer you're on and where you are. Glance at it before every command.

Look before you change

pwd, ls and whoami can't break anything. Use them to check where you are before doing anything that can.

Let it type for you

Tab and ↑ save time, and they save typos. A name Tab finished for you is spelled correctly.
