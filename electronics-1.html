How this guide works

A study guide, not a recipe

Each stage ends with a checkpoint: a few questions you should be able to answer in your own words. If you can, tick it and move on. If you can't, that's the part to go back over. Knowing why an LED needs a resistor is worth more than having followed the steps that fitted one.

The one safety rule

Batteries only. Nothing in this guide ever plugs into the wall. Mains electricity can kill you, and nothing here needs it.

Batteries can hurt you too if you're careless. Never connect a battery's two terminals straight to each other with a wire: that's a short circuit, and the wire and battery get hot fast. And don't experiment with the flat lithium cells from phones or vapes, which can catch fire if shorted.

Stage 0

What electricity actually is

Wires are full of tiny particles called electrons. A battery pushes them. When there's a complete path from one end of the battery, through your parts, and back to the other end, they flow round it. That flow is called current, and the path is called a circuit, from "circle".

A common way to picture it is water in a pipe:

  • Voltage is the push, like water pressure. Measured in volts (V).
  • Current is how much flows past each second, like litres per second. Measured in amps (A), or thousandths of an amp, milliamps (mA).
  • Resistance is how hard something makes it to flow, like a narrow pipe. Measured in ohms (Ω).

The picture isn't perfect, but it gets you a long way.

Experiment on the bench

Switch the circuit on and watch the dots. Now switch it off. The switch is at the top, nowhere near the LED at the bottom. Why does the LED go out anyway?

Why

Because it's a loop. Break it anywhere and the flow stops everywhere, all at once. A switch doesn't "block the electricity going to the LED". It opens a gap in the one path the current has. That's why the bulbs in an old string of fairy lights all went out when one failed.

Checkpoint

Can you explain, in your own words:

  • what voltage, current and resistance are,
  • why a circuit has to be a complete loop?

Stage 1

First light

An LED, a light-emitting diode, is a tiny lamp that only works one way round. One leg is longer: that's the positive leg, and it goes towards the battery's +. The rim of the LED also has a flat side, next to the negative leg.

Predict first

On the bench, set the resistor to None (a plain wire). Before you flick the switch: what do you think will happen?

What happens, and why

The LED burns out. With nothing to slow it down, far too much current flows, and the LED is destroyed in a flash. With a real LED, that's a pop, a smell, and a dead part.

That's what the resistor is for: it limits the current to a level the LED can take, about 20 mA for an ordinary one. This is the most common beginner's mistake, which is why it's the first thing to try here, where it's free.

With real parts

A breadboard lets you build circuits by pushing parts into holes, with no soldering. Inside, each short row of five holes is joined together, so two legs pushed into the same row are connected. The long lines of holes along the edges, often marked red and blue, are joined along their whole length. They're for the battery's + and −.

  1. Plug the battery clip's red wire into the red edge line and the black wire into the blue one. Don't connect the battery yet.
  2. Put a 470 Ω resistor from the red line into a row in the middle.
  3. Put the LED's long leg in that same row, and its short leg in the next row along.
  4. Run a jumper wire from that second row to the blue line.
  5. Now connect the battery.

Not lit? Turn the LED round. Every beginner puts one in backwards, and it does no harm: it simply doesn't light.

Checkpoint

Can you explain:

  • why an LED needs a resistor,
  • why the LED only works one way round?

Stage 2

The multimeter

A multimeter lets you see what's happening instead of guessing. It has a dial and usually three sockets. The black lead always goes in the socket marked COM. Where the red lead goes depends on what you're measuring.

Voltage: touch the two points

Put the red lead in the socket marked V, and turn the dial to DC volts (the V with a straight line over it, set to 20 if your meter has ranges). Touch the leads either side of whatever you're measuring: across the battery, across the resistor, across the LED.

Resistance: out of the circuit

Turn the dial to Ω and touch the resistor's two legs. Take it out of the circuit first, or the rest of the circuit confuses the reading. This is also how to identify a loose resistor that's lost its label.

Current: become part of the loop

Current is different. You can't measure flow by touching the outside of the pipe. You have to make the water go through the meter. Unplug one wire in the loop, then use the meter's two leads to bridge the gap. Move the red lead to the socket marked mA, and turn the dial to DC milliamps.

The trap that blows the fuse

After measuring current, move the red lead back to V. In the mA socket the meter is almost a plain wire. Touch it across a battery to "measure the voltage" and you've made a short circuit through the meter. The fuse inside blows, and the meter reads nothing until you replace it. Everyone does this once.

Predict first

On the bench with a 9 V battery, a 470 Ω resistor and a red LED: add up the voltage across the resistor and the voltage across the LED. What do you think the total will be?

What you should find

Exactly the battery voltage. The battery's push gets shared out round the loop, and the shares always add up to the whole. Try other resistors: the split changes, but the total never does. Engineers call this Kirchhoff's voltage law, but you've just seen it for yourself.

Checkpoint

Can you explain:

  • why you measure voltage across a part, but current through the loop,
  • which socket the red lead goes in for each, and why forgetting that blows a fuse?

Stage 3

Ohm's law, discovered

Here's the one relationship everything else in electronics is built on. Instead of being told it, fill in this table from the bench. Use the 9 V battery and a red LED, and for each resistor write down the voltage across the resistor and the current. Then divide one by the other.

To turn milliamps into amps, divide by 1,000. So 15 mA is 0.015 A.

Look at the last column

What do you notice about the numbers in the last column, compared with the first?

What you've found

Volts divided by amps gives you back the resistor's value, every time. That's Ohm's law: voltage = current × resistance, or V = I × R. (Current gets the letter I, from the French intensité du courant, the term used when the idea was first worked out.)

It's useful because it works backwards. To choose a resistor for a red LED on 9 V: the LED takes about 2 V, so the resistor must take the other 7 V. You want about 15 mA, which is 0.015 A. So R = 7 ÷ 0.015 ≈ 470 Ω, which is why this page started with 470 Ω.

One more experiment

Set the battery to 2 × AA (3 V) and the LED to White, with 100 Ω. It barely lights, or doesn't light at all. Red on the same battery works fine. Using what you now know, why?

Why

Different colours need different pushes before they light at all: about 2 V for red, about 3 V for white and blue. A white LED on a 3 V battery leaves almost nothing over for the resistor, so almost no current flows.

Checkpoint

Can you explain:

  • what Ohm's law says, in words,
  • how to work out the resistor for an LED on a given battery?

What I'd want you to take away

It's always a loop

Current needs a complete path. Break it anywhere and everything stops. When a circuit doesn't work, look for the gap.

Measure, don't guess

Voltage across a part, current through the loop. A meter turns an argument about what's happening into a reading.

V = I × R

You found it yourself from three readings. It picks your resistors, and it explains most of what you'll see from here.
