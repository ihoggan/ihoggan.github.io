Do you need a TV licence?

Once you start pulling radio and TV signals out of the air with your own kit, the question comes up fast. The answer surprises a lot of people: it isn't about owning a television. It's about what you watch, and whether it's live. This page sets out the rules as TV Licensing itself describes them, with links to every source, a quick checklist, and where a home radio receiver fits in.

Read this first

I'm not a lawyer, and this isn't legal advice. It's a plain summary of TV Licensing's own guidance as it read on 4 November 2026, with links so you can check it yourself. Rules change, so if you're making a decision, read the source pages, which are linked below.

Part 1

What needs a licence

According to TV Licensing, you need a TV licence to:

  • Watch or record live TV on any channel, as it's being broadcast. That covers any device: a TV, laptop, phone, tablet or games console. It also covers any service, including live channels on streaming and pay-TV services.
  • Watch live TV channels streamed on YouTube or similar sites, such as a news channel's live feed, and live sport or news on streaming services.
  • Use BBC iPlayer at all, whether live or on demand, on any device.
  • Watch +1 channels, repeats as they're broadcast, and live Red Button programmes. These all count as live TV.

One licence covers a whole household, however many devices are in it.

Predict first

You never watch TV as it's broadcast, but you catch up on one BBC drama on iPlayer a week later. Licence or no licence?

The answer

Licence. iPlayer is the exception to the "live" rule: using it at all needs one, even for catch-up. The same programme on DVD wouldn't.

Part 2

What doesn't

TV Licensing lists these as fine without a licence:

  • Catch-up and on-demand on services other than BBC iPlayer, such as ITVX, Channel 4's and Channel 5's apps, Netflix, Prime Video, Disney+ and similar, as long as it isn't live.
  • YouTube videos, including streamers' own videos, live or recorded. It's live TV channels on YouTube that need a licence, not people streaming their own content.
  • S4C on demand, rented or bought films, DVDs and Blu-rays, and video games.
  • Radio. TV Licensing says you can listen to BBC radio, and to audio on BBC Sounds, without one.

And owning a TV set, by itself, isn't what the licence is for. A television used only for films, games and catch-up from other services doesn't need one.

Part 4

Where a home radio receiver fits

This site's Radio path uses a USB radio stick to see what's in the air and build a window aerial. So, as I read the rules:

  • Listening to radio, FM or digital, with any kit, is fine. Radio doesn't need a TV licence.
  • Seeing TV signals on a spectrum chart, as in Got my ears on, isn't watching anything. The USB stick can't even decode digital TV. It only measures how strong each channel is.
  • A proper TV tuner, such as a TV board for a Raspberry Pi, would let you watch or record live TV. Using it for that is exactly what needs a licence. The kit doesn't decide it; what you do with it does.

Separately from TV licensing, the UK has rules about which radio messages you may listen to at all. Those are in part 1.

Part 5

If you don't need one

TV Licensing asks you to tell them, using its No Licence Needed declaration. If you already have a licence, you cancel it first, and you may be able to get a refund for the months left. According to its own page:

  • It may visit to confirm what you've told it, and says that around 1 in 14 people who declare they don't need a licence turn out to need one.
  • Watching without a licence when you need one risks prosecution and a fine of up to £1,000, plus costs.

If you do need one, TV Licensing lists the cost as £180 a year for colour (£60.50 for black and white). People aged 75 or over who receive Pension Credit can get one free.

The short version

Live TV on anything, or BBC iPlayer for anything: licence. Everything else in this list: no licence. When in doubt, use TV Licensing's own checker.
