pandas with penguins

In step 1, NumPy gave you rows of numbers, but you had to remember that column 4 was flippers and column 5 was weight. pandas gives the data a proper home: a table with named columns, words and numbers side by side, and a way to ask questions like "which island has the heaviest penguins?" in one line. By the end you'll have answered that, and found out why the answer is a trap.

Part 1

The whole table, at once

That's a DataFrame: a table, like a spreadsheet. Every column has a name. The numbers down the left (0, 1, 2…) are the index, pandas' own row numbers. Notice that species, island and sex are words, sitting happily next to numbers. NumPy couldn't do that.

Part 2

Find the gaps first

Step 1's big lesson: before you trust anything, ask what's missing. pandas does it for every column at once:

Reading the answer

Four measurement columns are each missing 2: the same two birds, never measured at all. And sex is missing 11: those birds were measured, but nobody recorded whether they were male or female. That's 13 gaps of two different kinds. And the decimal mystery: a whole-number column can't hold "missing", so pandas switched it to decimals to use NaN, the same "not a number" as step 1.

Look at the two unmeasured birds:

That line has the same shape as a NumPy mask: penguins["body_mass_g"].isna() gives True or False for each row, and putting it inside penguins[...] keeps only the Trues.

Part 3

Deciding what to do about them

There's no single right answer, and that's the point. You choose, and you say what you chose.

  • The two unmeasured birds are no use for anything about size. Drop them.
  • The eleven unsexed birds still have good measurements. Dropping them throws away real data. Keeping them means any question about sex has to skip them.
Predict first

If you drop every row with any gap, how many penguins are left? And if you only drop rows with no weight?

333 against 342. For this path, we'll keep it simple and use the fully complete rows, so every later step works on the same 333 penguins. Make the clean table, and add a column in kilograms while you're at it:

Lesson

Cleaning is a decision, not a chore. Write down what you dropped and why. "333 of 344, dropping 11 with no sex recorded and 2 never measured" is part of your result.

Part 4

Asking questions

One column on its own is a Series. The most useful question to ask a column of words is "how many of each?":

Predict first

Three species: Adelie, Chinstrap and Gentoo. Which do you think was counted most?

Now the big one. groupby splits the table into groups, does a sum on each, and puts the answers back together. Average weight by species:

Gentoos are the heavyweights, at about 5.1 kg against 3.7 kg for the other two. Now try it by island:

Think about it

Biscoe's penguins average about a kilogram heavier than the other islands'. Does Biscoe make penguins heavy? Better food? Before you answer, run the next box.

The trap

A crosstab counts every combination. Gentoos live only on Biscoe, and Chinstraps only on Dream. Biscoe isn't heavy because of the island. It's heavy because that's where the heavy species lives. Compare like with like, Adelies on all three islands, and the difference almost disappears. Try it: clean[clean["species"] == "Adelie"].groupby("island")["mass_kg"].mean().

This is called a confounder: a hidden third thing (species) that makes two others (island and weight) look connected. It fools newspapers, scientists and, in step 4, machine-learning models.

Part 5

Your turn

Challenge

Are males heavier than females, in every species? You can group by two columns at once: groupby(["species", "sex"]). Write it in the empty box.

One way to do it 
clean.groupby(["species", "sex"])["mass_kg"].mean().round(2)

Yes, in all three. Male Gentoos average about 5.5 kg against 4.7 kg for females, and the gap is a bit smaller in the other two species. Notice we could only ask this because we used clean, where every bird has its sex recorded.

And describe(), which summarises every number column at once: count, average, spread, smallest, largest, and the quarter-way points.

Check

You can load a CSV, count the gaps, make a clean table, add a column, and use groupby and crosstab to ask questions, and you know why "heaviest island" was misleading.

On your own machine

Run it without the browser

If you set up the folder in step 1, pandas is already installed. Otherwise follow that box first. Then:

cd ~/code/data-path && source venv/bin/activate
python3                  # then paste the boxes in, in order

In a terminal, tables print in plain text and may wrap if the window is narrow. Make the window wider, or try pip install jupyterlab and jupyter lab for a notebook in your browser that draws tables neatly.

What I'd want you to take away

Names, not numbers

A DataFrame keeps every column named, and words next to numbers.

Cleaning is a choice

Count the gaps, decide what to drop, and write down why.

Look for the hidden third thing

Biscoe looked heavy because of who lives there. Always ask what else differs between your groups.
